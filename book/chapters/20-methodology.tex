\chapter{What the Programme Learned About Method}
\label{ch:methodology}

\begin{record}
\textbf{What this chapter is.} Chapter~\ref{ch:house-rules} states the rules
and the failure each was a reaction to. This chapter asks the harder question:
\emph{which mechanisms actually caught things}, which caught nothing, and
which failure modes recurred even after a rule was written against them.

\smallskip
The answer is uncomfortable in one specific way. The programme's verification
apparatus has a good record --- it caught an inverted lemma, a cancelled term,
a wrong enstrophy assumption, a misread numerical signal, three summary
overstatements, three formulas that were not typesetting, a vacuous
specification and a refuted strategic steer. It produced no theorems. And the
single failure mode the rules exist to prevent --- \emph{reading a summary
instead of the document} --- recurred at \Sn{48}, in the work of the agent
that was enforcing the rules.
\end{record}

\section{The delegation architecture}
\label{sec:delegation}

The programme ran as an orchestrator plus subagents, and the division of
labour changed twice in ways that mattered.

  & --- \\[3pt]

\medskip
Two details of the \Sn{39} arrangement generalise and are worth naming
separately from the gate.

\emph{Name the failure modes before the work starts.} The \Sn{39} brief
identified, in advance, that the crux would be whether \(\sstar\) transfers in
the direction the argument needs, and that the infinite-past budget argument
was a likely trap. Both predictions were correct: the transfer question became
hypothesis \textbf{(R)}, and the budget argument became
\texttt{prop:no-summable-budget}, a theorem that it cannot work. A brief that
names what it expects to go wrong converts a vague ``verify this'' into a
checkable question.

\emph{Scope to one item.} The \Sn{41} brief was explicit: attack the annulus
step and nothing else. The resulting diff touched one file in three locations
and \(864\) lines, which is a diff a human can actually read as a sceptical
referee. Every subsequent delegated session used the same constraint.

\section{The merge gate, and what it caught}
\label{sec:gate-record}

The gate is stated in Chapter~\ref{ch:house-rules}: nothing merges until the
orchestrator has independently re-derived the load-bearing computations by
hand. A self-report, however carefully written, is not verification.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\Sn{44}: the inverted volume factor}{S44: the inverted volume factor}}

This is the gate's clearest justification and is set out fully in
Chapter~\ref{ch:non-implication}. The submission's headline was favourable:
band absorption is strictly weaker than the \(c_K\) hypothesis, and the
programme's existing measurements already support it. The supporting lemma had
the volume ratio upside down. Corrected, the headline inverts.

Three things about the catch generalise.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
\item \emph{The checks that found it were cheap.} Re-derive the algebra from
  the definitions; run the formula on four random configurations; test the
  sanity case. Total effort: minutes. The stated form matched nothing
  numerically; the corrected form matched to machine precision.
\item \emph{The error announced itself internally.} It contradicted two of the
  same submission's own results, and that internal inconsistency is what drew
  attention to it. A submission that is internally consistent is not
  necessarily right, but a submission that is not is certainly worth a second
  look, and inconsistency is mechanically detectable in a way that
  wrongness is not.
\item \emph{The error was in the favourable direction.} So was the \Sn{35}
  bonus term. So were all three of the \Sn{47} summary overstatements. So was
  the \Sn{48} steer. Every unforced error this book records runs in the
  programme's own favour, and none runs against it. That asymmetry is not
  coincidence and it is the reason a verification step has to be a separate
  job from the derivation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\Sn{47}: three overstatements, all one session old}{S47: three overstatements, all one session old}}

\Sn{47} applied the rule that the proof document governs its own summaries ---
to its own commissioning brief, written one session earlier by the same
orchestrator. It found three claims the document does not support:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item ``the S31 audit (all seven grounds)''. There are \textbf{eight},
  D1--D8, and the omitted one is the first appearance of the programme's
  central obstruction. Chapter~\ref{ch:inheritance} explains why the omission
  was not neutral: for sixteen sessions the navigational summary did not
  contain the observation that the central difficulty had already been named.
\item \texttt{conj:lc-s44} described as ``a common \emph{weaker} target''. It
  is neither weaker nor stronger than \texttt{conj:K-refined}; the two do not
  compare.
\item ``\(c_K\) and band-absorption proved independent''. What is proved is
  that no implication follows from (A1)--(A3), over admissible test
  configurations which need not come from Navier--Stokes solutions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\Sn{48}: a steer of the orchestrator's own, refuted}{S48: a steer of the orchestrator's own, refuted}}

\begin{record}
The \Sn{48} pre-read for the work package that settled \textbf{(R)} asserted
that the programme's Type-I hypothesis is on the \emph{vorticity}, so that
building an \(\omega\to u\) bridge would be the real work.

\smallskip
The premise was false. Line~5565 of the proof document assumes \emph{both}
Type-I bounds outright --- the vorticity bound and the velocity bound. And the
bridge is impossible anyway: \texttt{prop:s48-bridge-fails} shows the
interpolation exponent is forced to \(3/5\), not \(1/2\)
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cz-log}, \S\ref{sec:bridge-fails}).

\smallskip
The session log records the diagnosis in the first person and without
mitigation: ``I had read the summaries rather than the document --- the exact
failure mode the house rules exist to catch. Recorded because the erroneous
steer is in the scratchpad and in my own messages.''
\end{record}

\begin{obsv}[The rule was written in \Sn{47} and broken in \Sn{48}]
\label{obs:rule-broken-next-session}
This is the most instructive single episode in the chapter, and it should not
be filed as a curiosity. The rule that the primary document governs its
summaries was installed one session earlier, by the same orchestrator, after
catching three instances of exactly this error. It was then broken
immediately, in the very next session, by the person who installed it.

\smallskip
The lesson is not that the rule is bad. The lesson is about \emph{where} the
rule needs to bind. It bound where it was consciously applied --- when
checking a subagent's returned work, when writing a walls document. It did not
bind where the reading was casual and upstream: forming a strategic view
before the work starts. A brief is written from memory and from summaries,
because that is what is at hand; and a brief is precisely the artefact that
determines what a subagent spends a session doing.

\smallskip
The mitigating fact, and the reason this is recorded as a success of the
system rather than only a failure of it, is that the subagent \emph{refuted the
steer} and the refutation was accepted. The architecture was right even where
the instruction was wrong, because the subagent was told to work from the
document.
\end{obsv}

\label{sec:ladder}

not overlap as much as one would hope.

\subsection{Why grep-based verification failed for forty-six sessions}

Until \Sn{47} the proof document had \emph{never been compiled}. Every prior
verification was brace counting. The problem is not that brace counting is
weak; it is that brace counting is blind \emph{by construction} to entire
error classes, and nothing in the process surfaced that fact.

\Sn{39} found the first instance: \texttt{conjecture} and \texttt{fact} had
been used as environments across several sessions without ever being declared,
so the file would have failed on first compile. Three of the five sites
predated \Sn{39}. Brace counting cannot see this, because the braces are
balanced.

\Sn{47} installed a toolchain and compiled: \(142\) pages, and three further
defects that only a compiler finds. \(\backslash\)\texttt{colonequals} and
\(\backslash\)\texttt{fint} were undefined at three sites, which means
\emph{three formulas were not typesetting at all}. A document nobody had ever
rendered had three invisible equations in it.

\begin{obsv}[The general form of the failure]
\label{obs:proxy-verification}
For forty-six sessions the programme used a \emph{proxy} for verification ---
a cheap check correlated with the thing it wanted --- and never measured how
good the proxy was. The proxy was not weakly correlated; for the specific
classes it covered it was exact. It simply did not cover the classes that
mattered, and its coverage was never written down.

\smallskip
The repair, when it came, was not a better proxy. It was to run the real
thing once. Everything the compile found had been present for many sessions
and would have been found on the first day the document was rendered.
\end{obsv}

\subsection{Why a structural check is necessary but not sufficient}

\texttt{scripts/check\_proof.py} was written in \Sn{47} and is the programme's
one piece of genuinely reusable infrastructure. Its docstring states its own
limitation: green is necessary, not sufficient --- it cannot see undefined
macros, which is precisely how the three \Sn{47} defects survived every
previous check.

Its most interesting feature is not a check but an escape hatch. A reference
with no resolvable target must be \emph{declared} rather than guessed, via a
\texttt{\% CHECK-PROOF: ALLOW-DANGLING} marker plus a visible red note in the
body. The reasoning behind that design is the sharpest methodological point in
the toolchain: \emph{a guessed cross-reference injects a false statement into a
mathematical document}. \Sn{47} resolved four dangling references and justified
each with recorded evidence --- the highest-risk one because the citing line
quotes the target theorem's displayed conclusion verbatim --- rather than by
plausibility.

\subsection{Two toolchain facts that each cost an hour}

Both are in Chapter~\ref{ch:house-rules} and both are repeated here because
they are the kind of thing that makes a working system look broken.

\emph{\texttt{cm-super} is not optional.} Without it \texttt{microtype}'s font
expansion aborts with ``auto expansion is only possible with scalable
fonts''.

\emph{Do not grep for \texttt{\^{}!}.} The \texttt{-file-line-error} flag
reformats \TeX{} errors as \texttt{file:line:} with no leading bang, so
\verb|grep "^!"| reports zero errors on a run that has five. This actually
happened, in the session that installed the toolchain.

\section{Failure modes that survived the rules}
\label{sec:surviving-failures}

\subsection{Detached background runs}

Three separate times in \Sn{34} and \Sn{36}, an agent detached a long
numerical run into a background shell and ended its turn; the run sometimes
survived and sometimes died with the session. The mitigation that worked was
not a prohibition but a change of trust model: \emph{query the database
directly rather than trusting a self-report}, and, when a process is confirmed
alive, block on it in the orchestrator's own shell rather than the subagent's.

The failure recurred in a new form at \Sn{43}, where a background waiter used
a \texttt{pgrep -f} pattern that appeared in the waiter's own command line, so
it matched itself, could never exit, and never fired. The recorded fix is to
grep the log for a completion marker plus failure signatures rather than
\texttt{pgrep} for a string the waiter itself contains.

\subsection{Claims marked ``mechanical''}

\texttt{rem:level-iteration} stood for five sessions labelled ``it involves no
new estimate''. \Sn{41} executed it and proved it cannot close
(Chapter~\ref{ch:annulus}). A claim marked mechanical is still a claim, and it
is the class of claim least likely ever to be checked, because nobody
volunteers to verify bookkeeping.

\subsection{Environments that are never exercised}

The book's own master file carried a malformed \texttt{record} environment ---
\(\backslash\)\texttt{colorbox}'s argument split across the begin- and
end-code of \(\backslash\)\texttt{newenvironment}, leaving the begin-code
brace-unbalanced. \TeX{} mis-parses that at \emph{definition} time and fails
only on first \emph{use}, which is exactly why it survived a build of the book
with every chapter empty. The note recorded at the time generalises: \emph{an
environment that is never exercised is never tested.}

\subsection{Warning counts that are true and misleading}

An agent reported ``0 LaTeX warnings'' for a build. That was true for
\texttt{LaTeX Warning} proper. The build carried \(113\) hyperref bookmark
warnings against a baseline of \(109\) --- four new, all from a single
unguarded math subsection heading, fixed with
\(\backslash\)\texttt{texorpdfstring}. The lesson is about baselines: a count
reported without the number it is being compared against is not a measurement.

\section{Specification errors are named as loudly as mathematical ones}
\label{sec:spec-errors}

\Sn{39}'s numerical track returned two runs with Gr\"onwall margins of
\(5.5\times10^{19}\) and \(2.0\times10^{14}\), reported \textsf{PARTIAL}. The
addendum states flatly that the runs were \emph{vacuous} and that the fault was
in the \emph{specification}: accumulating \(\exp\int_0^TA\dee s\) over a
twenty-time-unit window with \(A\sim O(1\text{--}3.5)\) gives \(e^{70}\), so
the margin could not have been met by any residual whatsoever. The underlying
trajectories were fine --- the \(H^{-1}\) residual peaked at
\(4.0\times10^{-3}\) for Taylor--Green.

The addendum names this as an error in the coordinator's specification, not in
the implementation, ``because the programme's convention is that spec errors
get named as explicitly as mathematical ones''. The windowed rerun reduced the
Gr\"onwall factor from \(\sim10^{12}\) to \(O(3\text{--}5)\) and produced
informative comparisons.

This convention matters more than it sounds. In a delegated architecture the
default attribution for a bad result is to the executor, and the default
attribution is usually wrong: a subagent that does exactly what it was asked
has produced a correct execution of a wrong question.

\begin{obsv}[What the discipline is for]
\label{obs:discipline-purpose}
Twenty-one recorded catches. Not one of them is a new theorem, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:house-rules} says the same thing in fewer words: verification
discipline is a way of not being wrong; it is not a way of being right.

\smallskip
What the table does show is a change in \emph{kind} over time. The early
catches are numerical bugs and sampling errors --- things a careful
implementation avoids. The middle catches are mathematical: a cancelled term,
a false assumption, an inverted lemma. The late catches are almost all about
the \emph{record}: summaries that lost a defect, citations that do not say
what they are cited for, a reason that does not support a conclusion, two
efforts read as one. As a programme accumulates results, the dominant error
class stops being ``the mathematics is wrong'' and becomes ``the account of
the mathematics is wrong'', and the account is what the next session acts on.

\smallskip
That is the argument for a book like this one, and it is the only argument for
it that does not depend on the programme having succeeded.
\end{obsv}
